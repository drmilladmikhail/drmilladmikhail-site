How to Deal With a Narcissistic Partner: Signs and Steps to Protect Yourself

Dr. Millad Mikhail · November 17, 2026 · 6 min read

It doesn't matter whether your partner is a “narcissist” by diagnosis. What matters is seeing the behavior clearly, setting your boundaries, and staying close to your people and to yourself. If you feel lost inside the relationship, that isn't a flaw in you; it means you deserve support.

“At first he treated me like I was the most important person in the world. Now everything I say is wrong, and every fight somehow ends up being my fault.” I hear this a lot, and it's usually followed by one question: Is he a narcissist? And how do I deal with him? In this article we'll talk calmly and clearly about how to deal with a narcissistic partner, and how to protect yourself without losing yourself.

First: what does “narcissist” actually mean?

“Narcissist” has become a very common word on social media, and we now use it for anyone selfish or difficult. But let's separate two things:

  • Narcissistic personality disorder: this is a real mental health diagnosis, and only a qualified professional can make it after a full assessment. None of us has the right to diagnose someone from a distance.
  • Harmful narcissistic behavior: this is what you're living through and seeing with your own eyes. And that's what matters here.

The key point: you don't need a diagnosis to protect yourself. Whether or not your partner has a disorder, behavior that hurts you deserves your attention and a response.

Signs of narcissistic behavior in a relationship

No single sign settles it — it's the pattern that matters. Pay attention if you see several of these happening again and again:

  1. A dreamlike beginning: over-the-top attention and talk of marriage within the first few weeks. In Chapter 4 of the book Why Do We Love What Hurts Us? we call this “love bombing.” Afterwards, the attention drops suddenly or turns into criticism.
  2. Making you doubt your memory: “I never said that,” “You're imagining things.” This is called gaslighting.
  3. It's always your fault: they blame you for their own behavior, and you find yourself apologizing for things you didn't do wrong.
  4. Punishment by silence: when upset, they disappear or ignore you for days, until you're the one who rushes to make up.
  5. Your feelings don't count: when you talk about your hurt, the conversation gets turned around onto them, or you're told “You're too sensitive.”
  6. A constant need for admiration: they have to be the best and the center of attention, and your success may irritate them instead of making them happy.

And if you feel you're walking on eggshells so as not to upset them, that's an important feeling — don't ignore it. You can also look through the signs of healthy and hurtful relationships (Arabic) on the site.

Why is it so hard to leave, even when you know?

You're not weak, and you're not stupid. In Chapter 5 the book explains that there are “ropes” pulling us back into a hurtful relationship, and the two strongest are:

  • The trauma bond: when the hurt and the tenderness come from the same person, the moment of making up after a fight feels so sweet that you become even more attached. It's the same thing we explained in love that comes and goes.
  • Hope for the “good version”: you're attached to the person you saw at the beginning, and you keep waiting for that version to come back.

And here's an important rule from the book: people change when they decide to — not when we wait for them.

How to deal with a narcissistic partner: 6 steps

  1. Stop trying to change them: put your energy into what's in your hands — your own reactions and your own boundaries.
  2. Set short, clear boundaries: for example, “If you raise your voice or swear at me, I'll end the conversation and we'll continue later.” Most importantly, follow through calmly on what you said.
  3. Don't get pulled into the justification spiral: you don't have to prove your point twenty times. A sentence like “I see it differently” is enough.
  4. Protect your memory: write down what happened, with dates, somewhere private and safe that belongs only to you. It helps you trust yourself when the story gets twisted against you.
  5. Don't isolate yourself: hurtful relationships grow in secret. Stay close to your friends and family, and talk to someone you trust.
  6. If you have to keep dealing with them (children or shared work): keep communication to what's necessary — calm, brief, and without a lot of emotion.

And note one important point: couples counseling isn't always the first step. If there's fear, threats or ongoing humiliation, professionals often recommend individual support first, to help you understand your situation and protect yourself.

A short exercise: hope and reality

In one column, write what you hope for from them (for example: “He'll stop putting me down once he calms down”). In the column next to it, write what actually happens. Then ask yourself: how many times did that hope come true last month?

In Chapter 5 of the book you'll find this exercise in full, along with the “decision scale,” which helps you see the whole picture on paper instead of going around in circles in your head.

The bottom line

It doesn't matter whether your partner is a “narcissist” by diagnosis. What matters is seeing the behavior clearly, setting your boundaries, and staying close to your people and to yourself. And if you feel you've lost your way inside the relationship, that isn't a flaw in you. It means you deserve support to walk the road with you.
