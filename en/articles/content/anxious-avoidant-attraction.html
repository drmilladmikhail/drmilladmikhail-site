Remember the question at the heart of the book: “Why do we love what hurts us?” In the book I say that a big part of the answer lies in one thing: the anxious–avoidant relationship. Two people are strongly drawn to each other, then fall into a cycle that deepens both the attachment and the pain at the same time.

Let's go back to Amir, whom we met in The Four Attachment Styles. Amir is a composite of many stories, not one specific real person. As soon as someone gets truly close and tells him “I need you,” he feels suffocated and pulls away. Now imagine Amir meets someone with an anxious style — someone who fears the person they love will leave, and who needs a lot of reassurance. What happens next?

Why are anxious and avoidant people drawn to each other?

The book puts it simply: the anxious partner sees in the avoidant one the calm and confidence they long for. The avoidant partner sees in the anxious one the attention and warmth they went without.

In other words, each sees in the other what they feel is missing. The anxious partner tells themselves, “This person is steady — not shaken like me.” And the avoidant partner feels, at least for a while, that someone truly sees them and cares.

But keep in mind: not every anxious and avoidant pair falls into this dance. And most of us aren't one pure style. Still, when the dance does happen, it follows a very recognizable pattern.

The painful dance: the cycle in 6 steps

In Chapter 2 of the book, the cycle is broken into six steps:

  1. The anxious partner senses distance, so they move closer and ask for reassurance.
  2. The avoidant partner feels suffocated, so they pull further away.
  3. The anxious partner gets more stressed, so they pursue harder, get upset and reproach.
  4. The avoidant partner shuts down completely or disappears.
  5. The anxious partner gets worn out and steps back a little, so the avoidant partner relaxes and comes back, tender and warm.
  6. The anxious partner is overjoyed by that warmth — and the cycle starts all over again.

Notice that each of them does what soothes them, and that very thing is what unsettles the other. The closeness that reassures the anxious partner is what smothers the avoidant one, and the distance that calms the avoidant partner is what terrifies the anxious one.

Why does every round make the attachment stronger, not weaker?

Here's the surprise. Steps 5 and 6 are exactly what the book, in Chapter 1, calls “love that comes and goes.” When someone gives you attention one moment and withdraws it the next, your brain becomes more attached to them. Psychologists call this “intermittent reinforcement,” the same idea that keeps people hooked on games of chance.

The moment of warmth that follows distance feels incredibly precious, so the anxious partner keeps waiting for it and puts up with a lot to get it. That's why the book says: every round of the cycle makes the attachment stronger, not weaker. It also helps us understand why a relationship full of pain can be so hard to leave — and can even feel like “the strongest love we've ever had.” If you feel you keep repeating the same story with more than one person, also read Why Do I Keep Choosing the Same Person?

How do you break the anxious–avoidant cycle?

The good news in the book is clear: this cycle breaks when at least one partner understands it. Both people don't have to change at the same moment.

  • If you're closer to the anxious side: the step is learning to reassure yourself, instead of always waiting for reassurance to come from outside. An example from the book: “When he's slow to reply, I'll wait an hour before texting again.”
  • If you're closer to the avoidant side: the step is learning to stay present even when you feel the urge to run. Another example from the book: “When I feel like pulling away, I'll say so honestly instead of disappearing.”

The point isn't that either of them is “bad.” An attachment style isn't a final verdict or a flaw in you; it's the way your brain learned to protect you. And the book says these styles can change with awareness, safe relationships and therapy.

There's one more important point the book returns to in its final chapter: someone used to this cycle may meet a calm, steady person and feel they're “boring.” But calm isn't boring — calm is safety, and your body needs time to get used to it.

A short exercise: where are you in the cycle?

Go back to the six steps above. Think of the last disagreement or stretch of distance in your relationship, and write down: which step was I at? And what exactly did I do?

Then write down one small thing you'll do differently this week when you reach that same step. In the book you'll find a full exercise called “My pattern in real life,” with deeper questions about your childhood and your relationships. And if you don't know your style yet, try the free attachment style test (Arabic) — an awareness tool; the result is not a diagnosis.

The bottom line

Anxious and avoidant partners are drawn to each other because each sees in the other what they feel is missing. Once they enter the dance, every round deepens both the attachment and the pain. But as soon as one of them sees the cycle clearly, they can change their step — and the whole dance begins to change. Take your time, and don't rush yourself or the other person.
