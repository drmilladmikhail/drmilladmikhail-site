The Four Attachment Styles: How They Differ and Shape Relationships

Attachment styles describe differences in how safe and close we feel in relationships. Common categories include secure, anxious, avoidant and fearful-avoidant. They're descriptions that help us understand ourselves — not diagnoses or fixed identities — and you can't judge someone's style from a single situation.

Amir is 30, successful at work, and liked by everyone. But as soon as a woman gets truly close and tells him “I need you,” he feels suffocated and starts pulling away without knowing why.

Amir is a composite of many stories, not one specific real person. And Amir isn't a bad or cold person. His behavior can be understood through attachment styles, but it isn't enough on its own to determine Amir's style. In this article we'll explain the four attachment styles and how to tell which one you're closest to.

What is an attachment style?

In the 1950s and 1960s, British psychiatrist John Bowlby developed “attachment theory.” He proposed that from our earliest years, a child learns the answer to a very important question: “When I need someone, will they be there?”

Later, researcher Mary Ainsworth ran a well-known study called the “Strange Situation” and observed that children respond to their mother leaving and returning in different ways. In the 1980s, other researchers carried the idea over to adult romantic relationships, and the four styles emerged.

The answer we learned as children can stay with us and show up again when we grow up and fall in love. But that link is not inevitable: later experiences — a safe friendship, a good relationship, therapy — can change the picture.

The four attachment styles

1. Secure: Often learned as a child that the people who loved them were there when needed. Comfortable with closeness and not afraid of distance. Can say “I'm upset” without drama, and listen to a partner without feeling attacked.
Inner voice: “I deserve love, and people can usually be trusted.”

2. Anxious: Often learned that love was sometimes there and sometimes not. So they worry a lot that their partner will leave, need a lot of reassurance, and may read any delay in a reply as rejection.
Inner voice: “If I don't keep watch, they'll leave.”

3. Avoidant: Often learned that needing someone was met with rejection or neglect, so they decided early on to rely only on themselves. They value independence highly, and when a relationship gets very close they feel suffocated and pull away — just like Amir.
Inner voice: “No one can comfort me but myself.”

4. Fearful-avoidant: Wants closeness intensely and fears it intensely at the same time. They move closer and pull away, and their relationships are often marked by strong ups and downs.
Inner voice: “I need you, but if I get close you'll hurt me.”

Most of us aren't one pure style. You may see something of more than one style in yourself, but there's usually one that shows up more, especially under stress. And if you notice you keep choosing the same kind of person, also read Why Do I Keep Choosing the Same Person?

A short exercise: which sentence do you tell yourself?

Go back to the four inner-voice sentences above and pick the one that feels most like you during conflict or distance. Write it down, and next to it write the last time you felt it.

Your attachment style isn't a final verdict

The good news: your attachment style isn't a flaw in you. It's the way your brain learned to protect you. Many studies suggest these styles can change with awareness, safe relationships and therapy.

How can you use this idea without judging yourself?

Notice repeated behavior and its context, instead of treating every late reply as rejection or every request for space as a sign of avoidance. One experience isn't enough to determine someone's style, and later experiences and relationships can affect how we approach closeness.

Does an attachment test diagnose a mental health problem?

No. A self-test is a tool for reflecting on your answers — not a diagnosis, and not proof that you or your partner have a fixed style. If relationships are causing ongoing distress or affecting your life, an assessment with a qualified professional can help you understand the context, rather than relying on a test result alone.

Does avoidant attachment mean the person doesn't love?

Not necessarily. Discomfort with emotional closeness can exist alongside a wish for love and connection. At the same time, describing an attachment style is no excuse for insults, control or harm.
