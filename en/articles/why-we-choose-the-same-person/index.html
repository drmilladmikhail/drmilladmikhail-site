Repeating Hurtful Relationships: Why Do I Keep Choosing the Same Person?

Dr. Millad Mikhail · November 13, 2026 · 5 min read

In short

Repeating the same hurtful relationship doesn't mean you're stupid, weak or just “unlucky”; there may be a pattern worth understanding. Familiar doesn't mean right for you, and intermittent affection, fear of being alone and the way we see ourselves can all make leaving harder. Understanding your choices doesn't make you responsible for someone else's harm; responsibility for harm lies with the person causing it.

Why do I keep repeating the same hurtful relationships? Why do I pick a different person every time and still end up in the same pain? This article looks at patterns in how we choose partners — familiarity, fear of being alone, and the way we see ourselves — with an exercise to help you notice what keeps repeating. Understanding your choices doesn't make you responsible for someone else's harm; responsibility for harm lies with the person causing it.

“I don't understand myself. Every time I say: that's it, next time I'll choose right. A year later I'm with the same person — just with a different name.”

Sara said this to me. Sara isn't one specific real person; she's a composite of many stories I've heard. The first partner was suffocatingly jealous and would disappear for days. The second was very sweet at first, then started belittling her in front of his friends. With the third, she knew within the first month he wasn't right for her — but she couldn't leave.

If you feel like Sara, I want to tell you something first: you're not stupid, you're not weak, and you're not just “unlucky.” There may be a pattern repeating itself that's worth understanding. The ideas below are possibilities to reflect on, not a definitive explanation of your experience or a diagnosis of you or your partner.

1. Repeating relationships: does familiar mean right?

Sometimes what we're used to feels familiar even when it hurts. Experiences of conditional love or unpredictable emotions can shape what we expect from relationships — but that doesn't mean our past inevitably decides our choices, or that every hurtful relationship comes from childhood.

Just because something is familiar doesn't mean it's right for you.

2. Why does on-and-off affection make leaving harder?

When affection alternates with disappearing or hurtful behavior, you may find yourself waiting for the moments of tenderness and clinging to the hope they'll come back. This article uses the idea of “intermittent reinforcement” as a frame for understanding that waiting — not as a diagnosis of addiction or the only explanation for why leaving is hard.

3. Self-worth and choosing a partner

If there's a voice inside you saying “I don't deserve full love,” treat it as a question to reflect on: does it make it harder for you to express your needs or refuse treatment that hurts you? That doesn't mean low self-worth causes the harm, or that a person who was hurt chose to be hurt.

4. Fear of being alone and staying in a hurtful relationship

Sometimes the idea of being alone feels harder than staying in a relationship that hurts. But difficulty leaving can have other reasons too, such as fear, financial dependence or shared responsibilities. Don't judge yourself or others from outside the experience.

Exercise: How can I notice the pattern in my relationship choices?

If this exercise feels suitable and safe for you, write down past situations or relationships without names or personal details. Next to each one, write: What attracted me at first? What did I usually feel? Was I able to express my needs and boundaries?

Look at your answers: is there a trait or feeling that keeps coming up? Use what you notice to understand, not to blame yourself or diagnose anyone. The full “My Relationship Map” exercise is in the book.

Is repeating relationships connected to attachment styles?

If you'd like to explore this, read The Four Attachment Styles. You can also use the attachment style test (Arabic) to reflect on your answers, or read about the signs of healthy and hurtful relationships (Arabic). These tools are for awareness — not a diagnosis or a fixed judgment of a person or relationship.
